Dice Atrium

Dice is a family of free, open-source apps I write for my own game table and share with other game masters. Each one does a single job: DiceAudio runs the sound of a session, DiceCombats runs the fights, and more are on the way. None of them needs an installer or an account, and your data stays on your machine.

See the apps GitHub

The apps

Two apps are public today.

DiceAudio

Music, ambiences and sound effects for a session

Put your music, ambiences and sound effects in one library, build scenes that follow what happens at the table, and run the whole evening from one window. Of everything here, this is the app I am most proud of. The audio tools I tried before writing it all fell short somewhere: a good catalogue you cannot add your own files to, a poor player, a subscription, or an interface that takes an evening to learn. DiceAudio works from your own files, has a real player, and is simple to drive live. It also works well outside the hobby, from escape rooms to amateur theatre.

  • Tagged library, with a downloader for YouTube, SoundCloud and many other sites
  • Layered scenes with contexts such as calm or brawl that crossfade between them
  • Scenarios: the ordered list of what you plan to play, grouped by adventure
  • Local HTTP API, off by default, so another program can drive playback

DiceCombats

Combat manager for any game system

Build creatures from the fields your system needs, track initiative and hit points during combat, and let the app take care of the rest: damage types and resistances, a combat log, and automations that call other programs when something happens.

  • Custom creature sheets with ten field types, reusable across creatures
  • Bestiary and player groups, with import from aidedd.org for D&D
  • Damage calculation shown in full before you confirm it
  • Combat log with per-creature statistics, exportable as JSON

Connecting the apps

The apps are not integrated with each other out of the box. What they share is a common language: DiceCombats can send HTTP requests when something happens in combat, and DiceAudio can receive them on a local server you switch on in its settings. Wiring the two together is a manual setup, done request by request.

In practice I mostly use this to drive things outside the Dice family: a sound sting when a player lands a critical hit, lights that change when combat starts, or a trigger for a private tool of mine. Anything that accepts an HTTP request will do, a smart bulb as much as a stream overlay. A tighter integration between the apps may come later. My own sessions do not need it yet, but the building blocks are there if you want to wire them yourself.
